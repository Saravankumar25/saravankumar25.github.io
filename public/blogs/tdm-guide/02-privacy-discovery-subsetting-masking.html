Sensitive data, privacy laws and policies

In the why-TDM chapter you saw why teams cannot simply copy production into testing. The main reason is simple: production is full of real people's private details. In the data basics chapter you learned what tables, columns and keys are. This section joins the two ideas. Before any tool can protect data, someone has to say what "sensitive" means, which laws care about it, and how a tool can recognise it among thousands of columns.

This is the vocabulary layer of TDM. Every later chapter (discovery, subsetting, masking, and all three tools) uses the words defined here: PII, PHI, PCI, policy, rule, data domain, classification. If those words are fuzzy, the tools will feel like a maze of unexplained menus. If they are clear, the tools will feel like different dialects of the same language.

2.1 Words to know first

Sensitive data
Any data that could hurt a person or a company if it leaked or was misused: identity details, health records, payment details, passwords, trade secrets. "Sensitive" is a business and legal judgement, not just a technical one.
PII (Personally Identifiable Information)
Data that identifies a person, alone or combined with other data. Examples: name, email, phone number, a government ID number, a home address.
PHI (Protected Health Information)
Health-related data that can be tied to a person: diagnoses, treatments, medical record numbers, insurance member IDs. Informatica describes its PHI pack as aimed at the healthcare and pharmaceutical industries (User Guide 10.5.10, p.79).
PCI (Payment Card Industry) data
Payment card details such as the card number and expiry date. Informatica describes its PCI pack as aimed at banking and finance (User Guide 10.5.10, p.80).
Identifier
A piece of data that points to one person. A direct identifier does it on its own (an SSN). An indirect identifier (also called a quasi-identifier) only does it when combined with others (zip code plus birth date).
Re-identification
Working out who a "nameless" record belongs to by combining it with other information.
Data classification
Sorting data into sensitivity levels (for example public, internal, confidential, restricted) so each level gets the right protection.
Data domain
A named kind of data, such as "Social Security number" or "First name", described by how the data looks and what its column is usually called. Both Informatica and Delphix use this word, with slightly different meanings.
Rule
In Informatica, the logic that changes a value: a masking rule uses a masking technique (such as substitution) with chosen settings. Rules are covered in the masking concepts chapter.
Policy
In TDM, a named bundle that says which kinds of data are sensitive and how to protect each kind. Section 2.8 shows the exact Informatica structure.
Data steward
The person who knows a data set's meaning and quality and answers questions such as "what is in this column and who may see it?".
Compliance officer
The person responsible for making sure the organisation follows laws and internal rules about data.

Ask one question of each column: if this leaked, could someone be harmed or could ShopSphere be in trouble?

  • customer_id is just a number the system made up. On its own it harms nobody. But it links to orders, so it still needs careful handling (more in the masking concepts chapter).
  • first_name and last_name tell you who the person is.
  • email and phone let a stranger contact, spam, phish or impersonate the person.
  • ssn (a government ID number) is the classic example: with a name and a birth date it is enough to open credit in someone's name.
  • date_of_birth is used by banks and call centres to "prove" who you are.
  • zip_code looks harmless. Section 2.4 shows why it is not.
Why it matters in testing

Production data is protected by firewalls, access reviews and monitoring. A test database usually has none of that. Many people can log in, copies get passed around, and some environments live with outside vendors or in the cloud. If a real SSN sits in a test table, it carries production's legal weight into a place with a fraction of production's security. That mismatch is the whole reason this section exists.

Delphix's own introduction makes the same point from the tool side: organisations face data breaches and pressure from regulators, and encryption helps protect data on disks and in transit but is, in their words, ill-suited for protecting non-production environments used for development, testing and reporting. Their answer is to replace confidential values with fictitious but realistic ones. (Delphix "Introduction to Delphix Masking", source page ↗).

A quick test you can apply at work

Look at a column and ask three questions: (1) Does it identify a person? (2) Would a regulator or customer be upset if it leaked? (3) Would a tester still be able to do their job if it held a believable fake value instead? If the answers are yes, yes, yes, the column is a masking candidate. Question 3 matters: if the column cannot be faked without breaking the test, you have found a design problem to solve, not a reason to leave real data in place.

2.3 The three families: PII, PHI and PCI

People in TDM talk in three abbreviations. They overlap, and a single column can belong to more than one family, but each one points at a different law or standard and a different industry.

2.3.1 PII: personally identifiable information

PII is data that identifies a person. The definition differs a little between countries and laws, but the everyday list is stable:

  • Names, email addresses, phone numbers, postal addresses.
  • Government-issued numbers: SSN (US), passport number, driver's licence number, national ID. India's Aadhaar and PAN are comparable examples.
  • Date of birth, age, gender, place of birth.
  • Online identifiers such as IP addresses.

ShopSphere's CUSTOMERS table is almost pure PII: first_name, last_name, email, phone, ssn, date_of_birth, zip_code and tax_id.

Informatica ships a ready-made PII policy pack. It lists 26 data domains, each paired with a default masking rule (User Guide 10.5.10, pp.78 to 79). Reading the list is a quick way to see what a vendor treats as PII:

2.3.2 PHI: protected health information

PHI is health data that can be linked to a person. The health data alone ("type 2 diabetes") is not what makes it PHI. The link to an identifiable individual does. A list of diagnoses with no names or IDs and no way to trace back is a different thing from a patient record.

ShopSphere is a retailer, so it has no medical records. To make the idea concrete, imagine a hypothetical ShopSphere feature, "ShopSphere Pharmacy", with a table PRESCRIPTIONS(rx_id, customer_id, diagnosis_code, drug_name, insurer_member_id). Then:

Look closely at what the pack contains

The PHI pack is mostly identifiers that appear in healthcare data: names, record numbers, plan numbers, dates, ZIP. It does not try to list every diagnosis. That tells you how privacy rules think: what makes a health record dangerous is that it can be traced to a person, so removing or masking the identifiers does most of the protective work.

Delphix organises its detection logic into profile sets. Besides the recommended ASDD Standard set, it ships legacy sets named "Financial - Legacy" and "HIPAA - Legacy" (Delphix "Out of the box profiling settings"). Delphix's standard set also has domains with explicitly medical names, such as MEDICAL_CODES, MEDICAL_DRUG and BLOOD_TYPE, and a domain called HIPAA_DATE (Delphix "ASDD standard profile set" listing). The words "HIPAA Date" hint at a rule HIPAA has about dates, which section 2.5 explains.

2.3.3 PCI: payment card data

PCI refers to the Payment Card Industry. The PCI DSS (Data Security Standard) is a rulebook created by the card brands for anyone who stores, processes or sends card data. It is not a government law. It is a contractual standard: merchants who ignore it can lose the right to take card payments, and can be fined by their payment partners. In TDM talk, "PCI data" means card number, card expiry date, security code, cardholder name.

ShopSphere's shared schema has no card column, but any real online shop would have one (or would store a token from a payment provider). If it did, it would look like 4539 1596 8210 2773. This is the number Informatica's own documentation uses as a masking example (User Guide 10.5.10, p.158).

The Informatica PCI policy pack is short: Account Number, Birth Date, Credit Card, Expiration Date, First Name and Last Name (User Guide 10.5.10, p.80).

Do not copy these numbers from the docs into your own tests

A card number from a documentation page may look fake and still be a real, working card by coincidence. When you need card numbers for testing, use numbers designed for testing (payment providers publish "test card numbers") or let a generator or masking tool make numbers that pass the format check but belong to no account.

2.4 Direct and indirect identifiers: why "just remove the names" fails

A common beginner idea is: "We will delete the name column and then the data is anonymous." It is not, and understanding why is the most important privacy idea in this section.

2.4.1 Two kinds of identifier

Direct identifiers

Point to one person by themselves.

  • Full name (mostly)
  • SSN, passport number
  • Email address, phone number
  • Card number

Removing or replacing these is the obvious first step.

Indirect identifiers (quasi-identifiers)

Harmless alone, identifying in combination.

  • Zip or postal code
  • Date of birth
  • Gender
  • Job title, employer
  • Rare product or rare diagnosis

Easy to forget, which is why they cause leaks.

Research on public records, most famously by the computer scientist Latanya Sweeney, has found that a large share of people in the United States can be singled out from just three facts: zip code, full date of birth and gender. The exact percentage depends on the data set and the year, so do not quote a number from this guide. Remember the shape of the result: three boring columns, one unique person.

2.4.2 A worked example with ShopSphere

Imagine a careless tester shares an "anonymised" extract with a contractor. Names, emails, phones and SSNs were deleted. What remains:

"Anonymised" extract: direct identifiers removed, indirect ones kept
customer_iddate_of_birthzip_codetotal_spent
10011990-04-1256000141,250
10021985-11-026000179,800
10031999-07-2368200127,430

Now suppose the contractor also has an outside list: a public club membership page, a leaked mailing list, a social media profile that mentions "born 12 April 1990" and a city. Matching the outside list against the extract on birth date plus zip code finds exactly one row. The contractor now knows that Asha spent 41,250 at ShopSphere. No name was ever in the extract.

  1. Take the extract.

    Birth date and zip code are in it.

  2. Take any outside list that has the same two facts plus a name.

    The linking columns do not have to be sensitive on their own.

    Result: the outside list's "Asha Menon" matches one row of the extract.

  3. Read everything else in the matched row.

    Everything in the row now belongs to a named person.

What this means for masking

Protecting a data set means looking at combinations. It is why the PII and PHI packs both include Birth Date, ZIP and State, not only names and ID numbers. It is also why masking techniques that move a date a little or blur a number (see the masking concepts chapter) exist: they weaken the combination while keeping the data useful for testing.

Free text is the other hiding place

Informatica's data domain page notes that a Social Security number might sit in an SSN column, in a column called SOCIAL_SECURITY in another table, and also inside a COMMENTS column (User Guide 10.5.10, p.73). A support agent typing "customer 123-45-6789 called about refund" into a notes field has just put an SSN where no column name will ever warn you. Delphix's Free Text Redaction algorithm exists for exactly this problem (the masking concepts chapter explains it).

2.5 Privacy laws and standards in plain words

This is not legal advice

Everything in this section is a plain-language orientation , written so you can follow a meeting and ask good questions. Laws change, have exceptions, and apply differently depending on where your company and your customers are. Treat every statement here as "roughly true, check with legal". The tool documentation does not teach law, so none of this comes from the tool sources, except where marked.

Why should a tester care about laws? Because when a court or regulator looks at a data leak, they do not ask "was it production or test?". They ask "was personal data properly protected?". A copy in a test database is still personal data.

2.5.1 GDPR in plain words

The GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) is a European Union law that has applied since May 2018.

  • What it protects: "personal data", meaning any information about an identifiable person. That is broader than a name and number: online identifiers and location data can count.
  • Who it applies to: organisations that handle the personal data of people in the EU, even if the organisation sits outside Europe. A ShopSphere selling to customers in Germany would likely be in scope wherever its servers are.
  • Ideas behind it: there must be a lawful reason to use the data (consent is one such reason); use it only for the purpose it was collected for; collect and keep no more than needed ("data minimisation"); keep it secure; let people see and delete their data.
  • Fines at a high level: the most serious breaches can lead to fines of up to 20 million euros or 4 percent of worldwide yearly turnover, whichever is higher. Lesser breaches have a lower ceiling.

The consequence for test data. Two GDPR ideas hit TDM directly. First, purpose: a customer who gave details so ShopSphere could deliver an order did not necessarily agree to have them copied into a test lab. Second, minimisation and security: copying a whole production database to a loosely guarded test server is hard to justify when a small, masked copy would do. The law also treats truly anonymous data (nobody can be identified any more) as outside its scope, which is one reason masking and subsetting are popular. Whether a particular masked data set counts as anonymous is a question for your compliance officer.

2.5.2 HIPAA in plain words

HIPAA is a United States law from 1996 that, through its Privacy and Security Rules, protects health information.

  • What it protects: PHI, health information that identifies a person (section 2.3).
  • Who it applies to: "covered entities" (health plans, healthcare providers, healthcare clearinghouses) and their "business associates", meaning vendors who handle PHI for them. A software company testing a hospital's billing system is typically a business associate.
  • Minimum necessary: people and systems should receive only the least PHI they need for their job. Testers rarely need the real thing.
  • De-identification: HIPAA describes two accepted ways to de-identify health data so it is no longer treated as PHI. One removes a defined list of 18 kinds of identifiers (names, detailed locations, most date elements except the year, phone numbers, record numbers and so on). The other has a qualified expert certify that the risk of identifying anyone is very small. This may be why Delphix has a domain called HIPAA_DATE, and why dates and ZIP codes feature so heavily in PHI discussions.
  • Penalties at a high level: civil penalties in tiers depending on how careless the organisation was, and criminal penalties for knowing misuse.

Both tools mention these regimes only briefly. Delphix's introduction lists HIPAA, PCI and GDPR as examples of mandates organisations must stay compliant with, and Informatica packages its PHI and PCI policy packs for healthcare and banking respectively (User Guide 10.5.10, pp.79 to 80). GenRocket's column-profiling page says that identifying PII early helps organisations meet requirements "such as HIPAA".

2.6 Data classification: how sensitive is each column?

Data classification means labelling data by how much damage a leak could do, so that you can apply protection in proportion. Organisations choose their own scheme. A typical four-level scheme looks like this:

A typical four-level classification (illustrative; your organisation's levels may differ)
LevelMeaningShopSphere exampleTypical test-data action
PublicSafe for anyone to seePRODUCTS.product_name, PRODUCTS.priceCopy as is
InternalFine inside the company, not outsideORDERS.order_date, ORDERS.total_amountCopy as is, or lightly vary
ConfidentialHarms people or the business if leakedCUSTOMERS.email, phone, first_name, last_name, zip_codeMask with realistic replacements
RestrictedSerious legal or financial harmCUSTOMERS.ssn, tax_id, date_of_birth, card numbersMask strongly, or never move to test

Two things are worth noticing. First, classification is about columns (and sometimes whole tables), not rows. Second, the result of classifying is a plan: each level maps to an action. A column with no label gets no action, so unlabelled sensitive data is the dangerous kind.

2.6.1 How the tools record sensitivity

  • Informatica. When you create a data domain you set a sensitivity level for all its columns, chosen from levels that the Administrator defines (User Guide 10.5.10, p.75). Separately, each column in a project has a Sensitive flag. The guide says the flag is "for reference only" and does not affect processing, and that assigning a data domain to a column marks it sensitive automatically (p.89). A different property, Restricted, does change behaviour: the integration service does not write a restricted column's value to the target (p.89).
  • Delphix. A domain ties a category of sensitive data (the terminology page's example is social security numbers) to the way it should be secured. The profiler assigns the domain to a column and, with it, the domain's default masking algorithm (Delphix "Discovering your sensitive data").
Sensitive flag versus masking rule

In Informatica, marking a column "sensitive" does not mask it. It documents your decision. Masking happens only when a rule is assigned to the column and a plan runs (see the Informatica data masking chapter). Beginners often tick "sensitive" and think the job is done.

Many laws treat some data as especially sensitive: ethnic origin, political opinions, religion, trade union membership, health, sexual orientation, biometrics. The Delphix ASDD Standard profile set has domains for these (for example ETHNICITY, RELIGIOUS_ORIEN, POLITICAL_ORIEN, UNION_MEMBERSHIP_NO, BIOMETRIC and MEDICAL_CODES), and the default masking method it shows for BIOMETRIC is NullValueLookup. For the most delicate data, blanking a value is often better than faking it.

2.7 How sensitive data is found

A real production database can have hundreds of tables and tens of thousands of columns. Nobody can read them all, and column names are often cryptic (COL_17, FLD_X2). So tools look for sensitive data automatically. At concept level they all rely on a small set of signals. 2.7.2 What Informatica does: data patterns and metadata patterns

Informatica describes a data domain using regular expressions. A regular expression (regex) is a compact text recipe for a pattern, for example "three digits, a hyphen, two digits, a hyphen, four digits". The guide separates two kinds (User Guide 10.5.10, pp.74 to 75):

  • A data pattern matches the values. Its example for Social Security numbers is [0-9]{3}-[0-9]{2}-[0-9]{4}.
  • A metadata pattern matches column names. Its examples for Social Security numbers are .*SSN*, .*SOCIAL* and .*SECURITY*.

A column joins the data domain if it matches any expression in the domain. When you run a data domain profile you choose to search the data only, the column name only, or both, how many rows to read (default 1,000) and a minimum conformance percent, since not every row has to match the pattern (pp.95 and 100). 

Data pattern     [0-9]{3}-[0-9]{2}-[0-9]{4}     matches   123-45-6789
Metadata pattern .*SSN*                          matches   SSN, CUST_SSN, SSN_NO

2.7.3 What Delphix does: profile sets, classifiers and domains

Delphix calls the detection step profiling. A profiling job examines metadata (column names and types) and possibly the data itself, and when it decides a column is sensitive it assigns the matching domain and that domain's default masking algorithm. It only covers the tables and files in the chosen rule set (Delphix "Discovering your sensitive data", source page ↗).

The logic is packaged in classifiers, grouped into profile sets. Four classifier frameworks exist, and they map neatly to our signals:

Delphix classifier frameworks (Delphix "Discovering your sensitive data")

2.7.4 One real example: how Delphix recognises an SSN

The ASDD Standard listing (Delphix "ASDD standard profile set", source page ↗) shows the SSN domain is defined by three classifiers. In our words:

  1. A PATH classifier.

    A regex on the column path catches names like ssn or social security number with variants such as social_security_no. Its match strength is listed as 0.67.

  2. A REGEX classifier on the data.

    Patterns for the nine-digit format. The dashed or spaced form (123-45-6789) is listed with match strength 0.7, while nine bare digits are listed with only 0.1.

    The lower number for bare digits fits the idea in the weaknesses table above: nine digits in a row are far more likely to be something other than an SSN.

  3. A TYPE classifier.

    The column must be a Number or String of at least 9 characters. This rejects tiny columns.

The excerpt I had does not explain how the strengths are combined into a final decision, so read the product page for your version before relying on the numbers. What matters for a beginner is the design: several weak clues, each with a weight, are combined to decide.

2.7.5 What GenRocket does: column names only

GenRocket's "Data Column Profiling" scans column headers, not the data. It checks whether each column name contains a keyword from a list: 102 built-in names plus any your organisation adds (for example user_email matches email, client_ssn matches ssn). It then shows you the matches to choose from (GenRocket "PII Discovery with Data Column Profiling", source page ↗). It is the "column name" signal on its own. The upside is that the tool never needs to read real values.

You will read many patterns in policies, so here is the minimum: [0-9] means one digit, {n} repeats the previous item n times, ? makes it optional, * means zero or more, and a backslash makes a special character literal (User Guide 10.5.10, pp.74 to 75). A data pattern says what a value looks like, not what it is: [0-9]{10} matches a phone number and also a ten-digit order reference. That is the source of false alarms (false positives); a real SSN column stored without hyphens that fails the pattern is a false negative. Dialects differ: Delphix patterns start with (?i) for case-insensitive matching.

Now the vocabulary promised at the start. A TDM tool needs one place that records the answer to "what is sensitive here, and how do we protect each kind?". Informatica calls that place a policy. 2.8.1 What each piece is

"A data masking component that describes the methods to maintain the privacy of specific types of source data". It contains data domains. You assign a policy to a data source in a project, and one policy can serve several projects (p.71).

Describes the functional meaning of a column based on its data or its name. It carries data patterns, metadata patterns, a sensitivity level, a status (enabled or disabled) and, optionally, preferred masking and generation rules (pp.73 to 75).

A ready-made policy shipped with TDM. There are three: PII, PHI and PCI, visible after installation (p.77).

2.13 Common mistakes and gotchas

Mistakes beginners make
  • "We removed the names, so it is anonymous." Birth date plus zip plus gender can identify people (section 2.4).
  • Trusting automatic discovery. It gives candidates. Review them, and add what it misses.
  • Forgetting free-text columns. Notes, comments and descriptions can hold names and ID numbers that no column name reveals.
  • Ticking "sensitive" and stopping. In Informatica the flag is only a label. A masking rule must still be assigned (p.89).
  • Assuming a law's definition fits every country. Ask your compliance officer which regimes apply to your data subjects.
  • Mixing up Delphix "rule set" with Informatica "rule".
  • Treating PCI as a law. It is an industry standard, enforced through payment contracts.
  • Copying documentation sample values into tests. Samples like card numbers can be real by accident.

2.14 Recap

Remember
  • Sensitive data is whatever could harm a person or the company if leaked. PII identifies people, PHI ties health facts to a person, PCI covers payment card data.
  • Direct identifiers point to one person. Indirect identifiers (zip, birth date, gender) identify when combined, so deleting names is not enough.
  • GDPR and HIPAA, in plain words, say: have a lawful purpose, use the minimum necessary, secure it, and de-identify where you can. This is the legal case for subsetting and masking. This guide is not legal advice.
  • Classification gives each column a sensitivity level, and each level maps to an action.
  • Tools find sensitive columns by column names, data patterns and value lists, plus type checks. The output is candidates for human review.
  • An Informatica policy contains data domains. A domain holds patterns, a sensitivity level and preferred rules. A rule is a technique plus parameters. Delphix uses domains, classifiers, profile sets and algorithm instances for the same ideas.
  • People decide: the compliance officer sets what is sensitive, the data steward explains the data, the TDM engineer builds and runs the policy.
Discovery, profiling and subsetting: the concepts

Imagine you have to move a flat. Before you pack, you walk through every room and list what is there: which boxes are fragile, which items belong together, which papers are private. Only then do you decide what to take and how to wrap it. Test data work follows the same order. First you look inside the production database (discovery and profiling). Then you take only what you need (subsetting). After that, you protect what you took (masking, covered in the masking concepts chapter).

This section teaches the two first steps as ideas, without any tool buttons. It builds on the data basics chapter (tables, primary keys, foreign keys) and on the sensitive data chapter (what sensitive data is, and how patterns and names reveal it). The hands-on tool chapters that follow are Informatica discovery and profiling, Informatica data subset and Delphix profiling.

2.15 Words to know first

Data discovery
The step of finding out what is in a data source: which tables and columns exist, which hold sensitive data, how tables relate. Informatica uses "data discovery" for the whole activity and "profile" for each job inside it.
Profile (verb: to profile)
A job that scans a data source and reports facts about it. Delphix and GenRocket also say "profiling".
Metadata
Data about data: table names, column names, data types, lengths, key definitions. The column ssn and its type "string of 11 characters" are metadata. The value 123-45-6789 is data.
Primary key (PK)
A column (or set of columns) whose values are unique for every row and identify it, such as customer_id.
Foreign key (FK)
A column whose values point to a primary key in another table, such as ORDERS.customer_id pointing to CUSTOMERS.customer_id.
Parent table and child table
The table holding the primary key is the parent; the table holding the foreign key is the child. CUSTOMERS is the parent of ORDERS, and ORDERS is the parent of ORDER_ITEMS.
Referential integrity
The promise that every foreign key value points to a row that really exists. A child row with no parent is an orphan, and it means the data is broken.
Data subset
A smaller copy of a database that still hangs together: fewer rows, same structure, referential integrity intact.
Driving table (driver table)
The table you start from when you pick a subset. The tool finds related rows in other tables starting from it. Informatica uses exactly this term for the starting table of an entity.
Entity (Informatica)
A driving table plus all the tables related to it. It is the unit that Informatica copies to a subset database.

2.16 Data discovery and profiling

2.16.1 What it is

Data profiling means scanning a data source with software and producing a report of facts about it. Data discovery is the wider goal: using those facts to decide what to mask, what to subset, and how tables connect. Informatica puts it plainly: run profiles to discover source data for data masking and data subset operations (User Guide 10.5.10, p.86).

A profile changes nothing: it measures, lists and flags. Unlike a building survey, its flags are guesses that people must confirm.

2.16.2 Why it comes first

Masking and subsetting are only as good as your knowledge of the data.

  • You cannot mask a sensitive column you did not know about. Missing one column of SSNs means a leak.
  • You cannot subset safely without knowing the relationships. If you cut ORDERS without knowing it points at CUSTOMERS, you will leave orphans.
  • You cannot choose a masking technique without knowing the column's type, length and shape.
  • Real databases are rarely documented. Old tables, renamed columns and half-forgotten fields are normal.
Why it matters

Both Informatica and Delphix describe profiling as the step that feeds the next one. Informatica says that before masking you can discover which columns to mask with the same rules, and before subsetting you can discover relationships between tables and the entities for the subset (User Guide 10.5.10, p.86). Delphix says that after connecting data, the next step is discovering which of it should be secured (Delphix "Discovering your sensitive data").

2.16.3 What a profile reports

Typically: each column's data type and length; value patterns and their share (99 percent of phone values are ten digits); ranges, minimum, maximum and the share of nulls; the most common values; sensitive-looking columns (email matches an Email Address domain); primary key candidates (customer_id is 100 percent unique); and foreign key candidates (ORDERS.customer_id matches CUSTOMERS.customer_id).

2.17 Metadata profiling and data profiling

A profile can look at two different things, and the difference decides how fast it is and what it can find.

Metadata profiling

Reads only descriptions: table names, column names, types, lengths, declared keys.

  • Fast, and does not need to read any real customer values.
  • Finds a column called ssn, or user_email.
  • Misses cryptic names such as FLD_X2, and cannot tell whether notes contains an SSN.

Data profiling

Reads actual values (often a sample) and measures patterns, counts and uniqueness.

  • Slower, because it reads rows.
  • Finds a column of SSN-shaped values even when its name is meaningless.
  • Needs access to real production data, which is itself a privacy decision.

2.17.1 Sampling: a look, not a census

Scanning every row of a table with 80 million rows would be slow, so profiles often sample. Sampling means reading only some rows. That is efficient, but it has a catch: a value that appears in only 1 row in 10,000 may not be in the sample. If a few rows of a mostly-clean column hold real phone numbers, a small sample may miss them. Informatica reflects this with options such as "Maximum Rows to Profile" and "Minimum Conformance Percent" (p.95): the second one is the share of profiled rows that must match the pattern before the column is claimed for the domain.

2.18 Finding keys and relationships

Subsetting needs to know which tables are parents and which are children. Ideally the database declares its primary and foreign keys and the tool simply reads them. In practice many databases declare few or none (for performance, or because the application enforces the links). GenRocket's documentation says this directly: custom referential integrity is added "when referential integrity does not exist between tables in the source database" but the user still needs to subset (GenRocket "How to Add Custom Referential Integrity to Imported Table Schemas"). Discovery fills that gap by inferring the links.

2.18.1 Primary key discovery

A primary key candidate is a column, or a small set of columns, whose values are unique. Informatica's primary key profile reports the percentage of unique values and lets you limit how hard it searches (User Guide 10.5.10, pp.92 to 93).

Its options and defaults: the maximum number of columns combined into a key (3), the maximum rows to profile (1,000) and a conformance criterion, the minimum percentage of unique values allowed (99 percent) (p.93).

Why would you allow less than 100 percent? Because real data is messy. If 99.8 percent of values are unique and a few rows are duplicates, the column is probably meant to be a key and the duplicates are data-quality bugs. Informatica notes that a primary key profile is useful to find such non-unique rows (p.92). The result table shows, per candidate, the table, the column or combination of columns, the uniqueness percentage, whether it is verified, any existing constraint and a status of verified, approved or rejected (pp.103 to 104). When you approve a candidate, TDM adds it as a key to the table in its repository, not in the source database. Rejecting a candidate does not affect the subset (p.104).

Unique does not mean a good key

ssn is 100 percent unique in the table above, yet it would be a poor primary key: it is sensitive, so masking will change it, and every table that used it as a link would need identical treatment. A human picks customer_id, the "meaningless but stable" identifier. Uniqueness tells you what can be a key. A person decides what should be.

2.18.2 Foreign key and entity discovery

To find parent-child links, Informatica's entity profile "returns a list of possible entities by comparing column names in the source tables" (p.93). In the guide's example, Employee, Employee_Detail and Employee_Address all contain an EmpID column, so the profile suggests Employee as an entity with two child tables. When you later subset, a filter on EmpID applies to every table in that relationship.

The profile options show what matters when matching columns (p.94): whether to compare using the datatypes the tool inferred or the documented ones, whether comparison is case sensitive, whether to trim spaces first, the maximum number of foreign key results to return and the minimum confidence percent (the share of foreign key values that match). A coincidental match is the risk: two unrelated integer columns can overlap by chance, so discovery proposes and a person approves.

One practical detail: if you choose the entity profile type and the physical database has no primary keys, the profile returns nothing. Run the profile as primary key and entity together, and it can discover both the primary and foreign keys even when the database declares none (p.93).

Delphix says its masking keeps referential integrity by scanning metadata and data to identify and preserve the primary and foreign key relationships between elements, so that data is masked the same way across tables and databases (Delphix "Introduction to Delphix Masking"). How keys are defined in a Delphix rule set is covered in the Delphix rule sets and keys chapter.

2.20 What is data subsetting?

Start with the problem. ShopSphere's production database holds eight years of history: tens of millions of customers, hundreds of millions of orders, terabytes in total. A tester working on the checkout screen does not need all of it. Copying everything to every test environment is slow, expensive and risky. Data subsetting means copying only a slice of the database, small enough to be cheap, and consistent enough to be useful.

Informatica describes the goal as a "small, targeted, and referentially intact copy" of production data for use in a nonproduction environment such as development, test or training, and gives the example of financial data for a specific region or time period (User Guide 10.5.10, p.112). GenRocket frames it the same way: production data may contain sensitive data, "you also do not need all that data for application testing, only a subset" (GenRocket "Synthetic Data Masking (SDM) and Data Subsetting Overview").

2.20.1 Why teams subset

  • Size and storage cost: smaller copies need less disk and money (GenRocket lists saved storage as a benefit).
  • Speed: a small copy refreshes overnight instead of over days, and tests and reports run faster.
  • Privacy: fewer real records copied means less exposure and less to mask (the minimum necessary idea from the sensitive data chapter).
  • Focus: you can pick exactly what a test needs, such as one region or one scenario.

2.20.2 A subset is not just "the first 1,000 rows"

Beginners sometimes subset by taking the first N rows of each table. That breaks the data: the first 1,000 orders belong to customers that may not be in the first 1,000 customers. The result is orphans: orders with no customer, items with no order. A real subset starts from a choice (a set of customers, a time window) and then follows the relationships so that every kept row has all the parents it points to. Everything in the next sections is about doing that correctly.

2.22 Driver tables and child rows

Now the core mechanic. Every subset has a starting point: a table and a condition. The tool picks the matching rows there, then follows the relationships to select related rows in other tables.

2.22.1 Informatica: the entity and its driving table

In Informatica, an entity "consists of a driving table and related tables". The driving table is the starting point; TDM finds the tables related to it from physical (declared) and logical (discovered or added) constraints, and adds them to the entity by default. You then define filter criteria on columns of tables in the entity (User Guide 10.5.10, p.114). The guide's example: Employee as the driving table, with primary key EmpID, three child tables holding an EmpID foreign key, and a fourth table, BankAccounts, linked through an account number from one of the children (p.115). The Entity Map shows the star on the driving table.

The Entity Map can show all tables, the parents or children of a selected table, or cyclic relationships, and you can disable a relationship; a table left unrelated is excluded (p.115). The guide advises running data discovery before creating an entity so the constraints exist (p.116).

The guide's own small example

An employee has ID 10 and you want everything about that employee. Employee is the driving table; Employee_History and Employee_Order are related to it by EmpID. Define the criteria "EmpID equals 10". After the plan runs, the target database holds one Employee row for EmpID 10, all of that employee's history rows and all of their orders (p.121).

2.22.2 GenRocket: the subset table and the hierarchy

In GenRocket's G-Subset, only one table in a configuration can have subsetting conditions (Where clause and/or row limit). Rows that meet the condition are included, "all records in related tables associated with those meeting the Where Clause condition will be inserted as well" (Subsetting how-to). The Traverse Full Hierarchy page shows what "related" means. Its example database has Organization (1 row), Department (4), Employee (120), Address, Attendance, Payroll and Qualification (120 each) and Project (8). The condition is on Payroll: hours > 10, limited to 10 rows.

  • Default behaviour: G-Subset goes up from the subset table to the top-most parent to build its queries, then migrates from the top down: Organization, Department, Employee, Payroll. Only the ancestor rows related to the chosen Payroll rows move: 10 employees, the departments they belong to, and so on.
  • Traverse Full Hierarchy enabled: it also brings the other children of those ancestors: Project (2 rows), and Address, Attendance and Qualification rows for the same 10 employees.

The same page notes that the option is only available when a subsetting condition has been added to a table, and that masking rules you set for those extra tables are applied to them too.

Choose the driving table by the test question

If the test is "does the customer page show the right orders?", drive from CUSTOMERS. If the test is "does the monthly report add up?", drive from ORDERS with a date filter and let the parents (customers) and children (items) follow. The table you drive from decides which rows are complete and which are partial.

2.23 Keeping referential integrity

Referential integrity is the rule that every foreign key value points to a row that exists. It is the one property a subset must never lose. If it breaks, the application crashes or shows empty pages, and testers blame the data. GenRocket's page defines it in the same plain terms: it "refers to the accuracy and consistency of the defined relationships" between parent and child tables (GenRocket "How to Add Custom Referential Integrity to Imported Table Schemas").

2.23.1 How a naive subset breaks it

Suppose someone takes the first 6 rows of ORDERS and the first 4 rows of CUSTOMERS "to keep it small". Order 5006 belongs to customer 1005, who is the fifth customer and was left out. Order 5006 is now an orphan.

SELECT o.order_id, o.customer_id
FROM   ORDERS o
LEFT JOIN CUSTOMERS c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
WHERE  c.customer_id IS NULL;      -- any row returned is an orphan order

On the naive subset this query returns one row (order 5006, customer 1005). On a correct subset it returns nothing. The same check works one level down (ORDER_ITEMS to ORDERS, and ORDER_ITEMS to PRODUCTS). Run these checks after every subset: they are cheap and catch the commonest failure.

2.23.2 Integrity options in Informatica plans

Informatica goes beyond "no orphans". It also offers several ways to decide how much extra data to pull to keep the subset coherent. You choose the option in the plan's advanced settings, so the same entity can yield different subsets (User Guide 10.5.10, pp.121 to 123). By default all constraint relationships in an entity are "major", which can pull in excess data; you can optimise relations so that the entity has the minimum number of major relations while still covering all tables (p.119).

2.25 Reduced copies and full copies

A subset is one way to make a test copy small. There are other ways to get a test copy, and each has a place. The comparison below is a general guide, with the Delphix statements marked.

A full physical copy is the most realistic but the slowest and most exposed. A subset is small and quick to refresh but may miss rare cases. A virtual copy looks full but shares storage with its source, so it is cheap to create, but it still holds all the data and needs masking.

Delphix describes virtualization as producing "a functioning database or filesystem copy that is lightweight and ephemeral" (terminology page), and its introduction claims that ten virtual copies can fit into the space of one physical copy. The pages I read describe only simple row filters, not key-following subsets, for Delphix, so treat "virtual copy" and "subset" as two different answers to the same cost problem: Delphix makes full copies cheap; Informatica and GenRocket make copies small.

2.27 Common mistakes and gotchas

Mistakes beginners make
  • Taking "the first N rows" of every table. It creates orphans. Always start from a driving table and follow relationships.
  • Trusting discovery output without review. Reject coincidental key matches, add missed sensitive columns.
  • Choosing a sensitive column as a key. A unique SSN is a poor primary key because masking will change it.
  • Assuming a percentage limit is random. The documentation excerpts do not say so; verify.
  • Forgetting reference tables. A subset with an empty PRODUCTS table breaks every order screen. Copy lookup tables in full.
  • Filtering on a column you also mask. Decide whether the filter sees real or masked values.

2.28 Recap

Remember
  • Discovery and profiling come first: they report types, patterns, sensitive-looking columns, key candidates and relationships, from metadata (names and types) and from data (values, usually sampled).
  • Profiles guess. False positives waste effort or change keys; false negatives leak data. A human reviews and approves.
  • A subset is a small, targeted, referentially intact copy: cheaper to store, faster to refresh and test, and less real data exposed.
  • You choose rows by filter condition, percentage, row count or interval, time window, or by a driving table; tools also copy some tables in full.
  • A driving table is the starting point: related rows are followed down to children and up to parents so there are no orphans. Informatica calls the group an entity; GenRocket follows the hierarchy from the subset table.
  • Informatica offers transactional, referential-only, two-way and one-way integrity options that trade subset size against completeness.
  • Subsetting and masking are partners. Keep keys consistent when you combine them, and check for orphans afterwards.
Data masking: the concepts

You have found the sensitive columns (the sensitive data chapter) and cut the data down to a slice (the discovery and subsetting concepts chapter). Now comes the step that makes the copy safe to hand to testers: masking, replacing real sensitive values with realistic fake ones. This section explains the idea and the common techniques in plain words, with before and after values on ShopSphere data. The tool-specific chapters, Informatica data masking and Delphix algorithms and masking jobs, then show where the buttons are.

2.29 Words to know first

Masking
Replacing sensitive values with realistic but fictitious values so test data cannot identify real people. Delphix defines it as iterating through a dataset to identify all sensitive fields and replace them with desensitised values to eliminate risk in lower environments.
Technique (Informatica) / algorithm (Delphix)
The method that changes a value, such as substitution or date shift. Informatica turns a technique plus settings into a rule. Delphix turns an algorithm framework plus settings into an algorithm instance.
Deterministic
Always gives the same output for the same input (and same settings). The opposite is non-deterministic or random: a different output each time.
Repeatable
Informatica's word for deterministic output across runs and tables, switched on with a seed.
Seed / key
A starting value that controls the output of a deterministic technique. Same seed (Informatica) or same algorithm key (Delphix) gives the same masked values; changing it changes them.
Dictionary / lookup file
A list of replacement values, such as thousands of first names, that substitution picks from.
Collision
Two different inputs masked to the same output. Fine for names, harmful for a column that must be unique.
Irreversible
You cannot get the original value back from the masked one.
Format-preserving
The masked value keeps the original's shape: a 10-digit phone stays 10 digits, a card number keeps its checksum.
In-place and on-the-fly
In-place masking changes the data where it sits. On-the-fly masking reads unmasked data and writes masked data to a different place.

Anonymisation is a destination, masking is a vehicle

Masking is a set of techniques. Whether the masked result counts as anonymous under a law depends on the whole data set (the combinations you saw in the sensitive data chapter) and is the compliance officer's call.

2.32.3 Key masking

Produce a deterministic masked value from the input and a seed: same input, same seed, same output. Informatica's Key technique works for dates, numbers and strings, and you can limit each output character to letters, digits or alphanumerics with a mask format (pp.164 to 168). For numbers, the guide says to use the same seed in the primary key column and the foreign key column so referential integrity is maintained (p.167). Delphix does the same job with Character Mapping, which maps each value to another value built from the same character groups, giving no two inputs the same output, which suits primary and foreign key columns (Character Mapping page).

2.32.4 Encryption and tokenization

Informatica offers standard encryption (AES, CRC, MD5, with a key of 16 characters or fewer) and format preserving encryption. The latter can keep the format and length, and can be decrypted with the same pass phrase and configuration. Its example: Abc123@xyz.com becomes Mpz849#dje!kuw, or Mpz849@dje.kuw if the "@" and "." are excluded from encryption (pp.161 to 162). The Delphix Tokenization framework uses AES-128 and produces longer alphanumeric tokens, built for the vendor round trip described in 6.2 (Tokenization page). Because AES and format preserving encryption can be reversed, use them only for that.

2.32.5 Nulling and redaction

Remove the value, or part of it. Informatica's Nullification replaces a column with NULL, works on all data types and has no parameters (p.168). The simplest safe answer for a column the tests do not use, such as tax_id, is NULL. The cost: NOT NULL columns refuse it and the application paths that read the column go untested. Free text needs smarter redaction: Delphix's Free Text Redaction finds sensitive words and patterns inside a text column (for example "St", "Blvd", or the shape 123-45-6789 for an SSN) and redacts the whole matched word, but it "requires some expertise to use" (Free Text Redaction page).

2.32.6 Date shifting and blurring

Move a date by an amount so that the exact date is hidden but the date still looks plausible. Delphix Date Shift masks each date to another within a configured range (for example 3 to 5 days later), based on the algorithm key; a fixed shift (same minimum and maximum) preserves uniqueness and referential integrity, unlike variable ranges. Its docs show 2021-02-03 12:30:00 becoming 2021-02-06 12:30:00 (Date Shift page). Informatica blurs a date within a range of years, months, days or hours: a source of 2 February 2006 with a two-year bound becomes a date between 2 February 2004 and 2 February 2008 (p.170).

2.32.7 Numeric variance

Move a number by a controlled amount. Informatica numeric blurring uses a fixed or percent variance. With the input 66: fixed bounds 10 and 10 give a result between 56 and 76; percent bounds 50 and 50 give 33 to 99 (p.170). A range option returns a number between a minimum and a maximum. Delphix Min Max pulls extreme values toward the middle, because a record with the highest salary may reveal a company's CEO (Min Max Number page). On ShopSphere, a 10 percent blur turns total_amount 1200.00 into something between 1080.00 and 1320.00, which keeps totals plausible but breaks the rule "total equals the sum of its items" unless the items are changed too, so decide whether tests rely on that.

2.32.8 Format-preserving masking

The masked value keeps the format the application checks. Informatica has built-in formats. Credit card masking returns a number with a valid checksum, leaves the six-digit bank identification number unchanged, and can keep or change the card issuer: 4539 1596 8210 2773 becomes 4539 1516 0556 7067 (p.158). Phone masking keeps the format: (408)382 0658 becomes (408)256 3106 (p.169). SSN masking generates valid numbers that avoid ones the Social Security Administration has issued (p.175). Delphix Payment Card preserves the Luhn check and the leading digits you choose: 5419033646326699 becomes 5419036803270758 when the first six digits are kept (Payment Card page).

2.32.9 Character replacement

Replace characters according to a pattern. In Informatica's key and random techniques you set a mask format: A for letters, D for digits, N for alphanumerics, X for any character, + for "leave as is", and R for the remaining characters (pp.165, 170); and you can mask only chosen source characters or restrict the result to chosen characters. Delphix's Character Replacement framework swaps listed characters (for example vowels to "+") and wraps another string algorithm, and the page notes it has no UI or default instance at present, so it is configured through the API. A ShopSphere use: a tax ID shaped like AKPPM4821Q (five letters, four digits, one letter) masked with format AAAAADDDDA becomes something like TRWQD9037B, same shape, no real content.

2.32.10 Algorithm chaining

Combine several steps into one result. Informatica's advanced masking uses input, variable and output columns: mask FIRST_NAME and LAST_NAME separately with substitution into variable columns, then output FIRST_MASKED || ' ' || LAST_MASKED as the full name (p.158). Its email masking can build the address from the masked first and last name (p.159). Delphix's String Algorithm Chain runs two to eight string algorithms one after another, and its docs give a reason to chain: with deterministic masking, a very rare input value keeps its low frequency in the output and could be used to infer the original, so a first algorithm can alter such rare values before the main one runs (String Algorithm Chain page).

Why chain at all?

Real columns are related: the email contains the name, the full name contains the first name. If each is masked independently, the masked email will name someone different from the masked name. Chaining makes the dependent value follow the one it depends on.

This is the idea that separates amateur masking from professional masking. Imagine ShopSphere masks CUSTOMERS.customer_id randomly, and then masks ORDERS.customer_id randomly in a separate step:

Masking a primary key or foreign key is only safe if every copy of the same real value turns into the same masked value. The same holds across systems: if the billing database and the orders database both hold customer 1001, they must both end up with 48213, or reconciliation tests will fail. This property is called deterministic (Informatica: repeatable) masking, and referential integrity depends on it.

2.33.1 How the tools achieve it

  • Informatica seed and repeatable output. With repeatable output, "John Smith" is masked as "Frank Martinez" in every table, account number 1234 always becomes 6549 and 5678 always becomes 3214, across runs. A seed, a number from 1 through 999 with default 1, is the starting point; use the same seed on the same kind of column in different tables. An administrator can set a central seed that overrides all others, and a seed can come from a parameter file (pp.154 to 155).
  • Informatica cascades. An auto cascade masks a parent key and the related foreign keys in child tables with the same value, applied by default where key constraints exist (p.91). A value cascade does the same for similar columns that are not linked by a key, such as employee_num in one table and EmpID in another, by choosing a driving column (p.90).
  • Delphix key and domain. Most algorithms carry a key; the same algorithm with the same key gives the same output, and changing the key changes every output (the Algorithms page gives an example where "Michelle" becomes "Rachael" and after rekeying "Ben"). Because each domain has one default algorithm, every column found in that domain is masked the same way (Discovering your sensitive data).
  • Delphix across engines. Algorithms can be synchronised between masking engines so that two engines mask the same input the same way. Algorithms that cannot guarantee the same output, such as the variable Date Shift, SecureShuffle and the random Secure Lookup, are called non-deterministic and cannot be used for syncable masking, while a fixed Date Shift is deterministic (Algorithm syncability page).
Things that quietly break consistency
  • Different spellings of the same value. Informatica's key masking is case sensitive by default, so "john brown" and "JOHN BROWN" can mask differently unless you convert the source to one format first with a preprocessing expression (p.166). Delphix Secure Lookup has a case-sensitive option and whitespace options with similar effects.
  • The value hiding inside another column. A name in FIRST_NAME may also appear inside FULL_NAME or DESCRIPTION. Informatica's delimited string masking masks such substrings consistently, for example "Donne, John" and "John Donne, General Manager" (pp.166 to 167).
  • Random options. A "random" mode is fine for a column that nothing links to. The Delphix Secure Lookup RANDOMIZE method "does not maintain referential integrity and should never be used" where it is required.
  • Different algorithm or seed for the same data in two jobs. Keep one policy and one seed or key per kind of data (this is what a policy from the sensitive data chapter is for).
Repeatable output has a price

The same property that keeps joins working also means the mapping can be recomputed by anyone who has the seed or key and the algorithm. For a small value space (an SSN has about a billion possibilities) that is guessable. Treat seeds and keys as secrets, store them outside the test environment, and rotate them when policy says so (Delphix lets you randomise an algorithm key through its API; the Algorithms page gives the endpoint).

2.34 Irreversibility, uniqueness and collisions

Irreversible by design. A good masking technique throws the original away. Substitution picks an unrelated value; shuffle and key masking cannot be undone without the seed and source. Encryption and tokenization are the exceptions (section 2.30). Unique by need. Some columns must not repeat: primary keys, SSNs, emails used as login names. A technique that can produce the same output for two inputs (a collision) will break them. Choose by need:

2.35 Static or dynamic, in-place or on-the-fly

Two different choices are often mixed up. Static versus dynamic asks when data is masked. In-place versus on-the-fly asks where the masked data ends up.

Static masking

Create a masked copy once. The copy is stored, and from then on anyone using it sees masked data. This is what test data management normally does.

Dynamic masking

Mask at the moment of reading, depending on who asks. The stored data stays real, and an application or proxy hides values from some users. It protects production views, not test copies. The TDM sources I read do not cover it.

A word of caution on vocabulary: GenRocket also uses "dynamically" for masking that happens as rows are inserted into the destination, which is closer to on-the-fly than to the dynamic masking above (GenRocket "SDM and Data Subsetting Overview").

2.39 Common mistakes and gotchas

Mistakes beginners make
  • Calling encryption "masking". It can be reversed. Use it only when reversal is intended.
  • Masking keys independently. Joins break. Use the same seed or key, or cascades.
  • Ignoring the job warnings. In Delphix, non-conformant values are left unmasked by default and only flagged.
  • Trusting one technique for everything. Shuffle leaves real values in place; substitution can collide; date shifting can reorder dates.
  • Forgetting free text and indirect identifiers. They defeat column-level masking.
  • Sharing the seed or key with the test team. It makes the mapping reproducible by anyone who has it.
  • Declaring success without validating. Run discovery again on the masked data and check joins.

2.40 Recap

Remember
  • Masking replaces real values with realistic fake ones, usually irreversibly. Encryption and tokenization are reversible; anonymisation is a legal goal that masking helps reach.
  • Good masked data is safe, realistic, valid, consistent, repeatable and still fit for the tests.
  • Common techniques: substitution, shuffle, key masking, encryption, nulling and redaction, date shifting, numeric variance, format-preserving masking, character replacement, and chaining several of them.
  • Deterministic masking (same input, same output, from a seed or key) is what keeps keys and joins working across tables and systems. Random masking is for columns nothing links to.
  • Choose by column type, uniqueness, format and test needs. Not every column needs masking, and free text needs special handling.
  • Masking can be in place or on the fly; static copies are the norm for test data.
  • Always validate: rediscover, check joins, formats, uniqueness, repeatability and job warnings.
