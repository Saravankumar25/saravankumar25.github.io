2.10 Real-world scenarios at ShopSphere

Three short stories, each choosing a different mix of the six capabilities.

Scenario 1: weekly QA refresh

Situation. QA tests every Monday and needs realistic data, but production holds real customers.

Approach. Each Friday night a job reads production, picks the last 12 months of customers and their orders (subset), replaces names, emails, phones, SSNs and dates of birth with fake but consistent values (mask), and loads the result into QA (provision). Discovery was done once, and its results are re-checked when the schema changes.

Outcome. QA gets fresh, safe, small data every Monday without anyone touching production data by hand.

Scenario 2: a new loyalty-points feature

Situation. ShopSphere adds loyalty points. No production data has points yet.

Approach. Nothing to copy, so the team generates 10,000 customers with random point balances, including zero, the maximum and a negative balance that the app must reject.

Outcome. The feature is tested before launch, including the edge cases, with no personal data involved.

Scenario 3: performance test before the holiday sale

Situation. The company expects ten times normal traffic and needs 50 million orders in the performance environment.

Approach. Real production does not have that many. The TDM engineer generates the volume, and masks a real sample so that distributions (how many items per order, typical prices) match the real shape.

Outcome. The load test runs at realistic scale, safely.

Lab: choose the capabilities (thinking lab, no tool needed)

Goal: practise mapping a testing problem to the six capabilities.

For each situation, write down which of discover, subset, mask, generate, provision and govern you would use, and in what order.

  1. The UAT team complains the database is too slow to refresh and takes 6 TB.

    Think about size and speed.

  2. A developer wants to test what happens when a customer's email is blank.

    Think about whether such a record exists in production.

  3. ShopSphere acquires another retailer whose database has unfamiliar columns and nobody knows what is sensitive.

    Think about what must come first.

Show a worked answer

1. Subset first (cut the size). Mask what remains, then provision. If a virtual-copy product is available, that also speeds provisioning (Delphix describes this; the Delphix overview and terminology chapter).

2. Generate. A blank email is probably rare or absent in real data and may be rejected by production's own form, so create it on purpose.

3. Discover first. Until you know which columns hold sensitive data you cannot decide what to mask or subset. Then mask, then provision, and govern by recording the decisions.

2.11 Benefits and trade-offs

Benefits

  • Lower privacy and compliance risk.
  • Faster test cycles because data is ready.
  • Smaller storage and infrastructure cost.
  • Better test coverage through generated edge cases.
  • Repeatable tests on known data.
  • Fewer false bug reports from bad data.

Trade-offs and costs

  • Set-up effort: discovery, rules and approvals take time up front.
  • Licence and infrastructure cost for commercial tools.
  • Masked data is never exactly real data; some production-only bugs may not reproduce.
  • Generated data is only as good as its rules; it can miss real-world quirks you did not think of.
  • New skills and a new process for the team to learn.
  • Ongoing upkeep as the schema and the regulations change.
General concept
Masked and generated data compared

Masking starts from real data and hides the identifying parts, so it keeps real-world shape and messiness. Generation starts from rules, so it is safe by construction and covers cases you design, but only the cases you thought of. Many teams use both: masked data for realism, generated data for edge cases and volume. the classification and generation concepts chapter compares them in detail.

2.12 TDM maturity: from ad hoc to self-service

Organisations do not jump straight to full TDM. A common way to describe the journey is a ladder of stages. This is a teaching model, not an official standard; names and numbers of levels vary between authors.

General concept
StageHow data is obtainedTypical pain
1. Ad hocEach team copies production or types data by hand, whenever it can.Privacy risk, inconsistent data, long waits.
2. ScriptedSomeone writes scripts to subset or mask. Works, but depends on a few people.Scripts break when the schema changes; knowledge sits in heads.
3. ManagedA TDM team uses a tool with defined rules: discovery, masking, subsetting, scheduled refreshes, approvals.Requests still go through the team, which becomes a bottleneck.
4. Self-service and automatedTesters request or refresh data themselves through a portal or API, often inside the build pipeline; governance is automatic.Needs investment and discipline to keep rules current.

The product documentation points toward the top of the ladder. Informatica mentions a self-service portal for users who use test data warehouse data but do not create it (User Guide 10.5.10, p.22). Delphix describes a self-service service where testers can refresh, rewind, bookmark and share copies. From docs Both are vendor views of what later stages look like, not proof of how any particular organisation works.

Why maturity matters to you

When you join a project, find out which stage it is in. A stage 1 team needs convincing and a small pilot. A stage 3 team needs a good TDM engineer to keep the machine running. 2.13 The commercial TDM product landscape

What "COTS" means

COTS stands for commercial off-the-shelf. It means software that a vendor builds once and sells to many customers, as opposed to custom software written for one company. You buy a licence, install it, and configure it with your own rules.

General concept

In TDM there are three broad ways to get the capabilities from section 2.6:

ApproachStrengthsWeaknesses
Home-grown scriptsCheap to start; tailored to your database.Hard to maintain; usually weak on discovery, governance and audit evidence; depends on key people.
Open-source toolsNo licence fee; community knowledge.You assemble and support it yourself; coverage varies.
COTS productsMany capabilities in one supported product; connectors to many databases; auditing and role-based access; vendor support.Licence cost; training; you follow the vendor's way of working; version upgrades.

The comparison is general; real products vary, so evaluate with a proof of concept (a small trial on your own data). The planning real engagements chapter shows how.

2.14 Common mistakes and gotchas

"We mask it, so it is safe"

Masking only protects the columns you masked. If discovery missed a column, or a free-text "notes" field contains names, sensitive data survives. Discovery and verification matter as much as the masking itself.

Treating TDM as a one-off project

Production changes every day: new columns, new data, new rules. A TDM set-up that is never maintained goes stale just like a copied database.

Using one technique for everything

Masking is not always right. If no real data exists for the case, or real data cannot be used at all, generate. If the problem is only size or speed, subset or virtualise.

Ignoring relationships

Any change to the data (subset, mask, generate) must keep related tables consistent. The data basics chapter explains how; skipping it leads to the "orders with no customer" bug.

Believing tool names are the concepts

"Plan", "rule set", "scenario" and "domain" mean different things in different products. Learn the concept, then learn each tool's word for it.

2.15 Recap

Recap
  • Testing needs data; good test data is realistic, consistent, complete, right-sized, safe, available and repeatable.
  • Copying production gives real data but causes privacy leaks, huge size, slow refreshes, stale data, missing edge cases, broken relationships, cost and loss of control.
  • TDM is the set of processes, roles and tools that deliver safe, right-sized, realistic test data on demand.
  • Its six capabilities are discover, subset, mask, generate, provision and govern; most projects use several.
  • Roles include testers, developers, the TDM engineer, data steward, compliance officer, DBA and administrators.
  • Maturity runs from ad hoc, through scripted and managed, to self-service and automated.
  • COTS means commercial off-the-shelf; the programme uses Informatica TDM, GenRocket and Delphix. Ask the trainer about "partnership" and "Cog IPs".
