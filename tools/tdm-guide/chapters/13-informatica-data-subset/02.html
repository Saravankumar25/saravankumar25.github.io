13.4 Entities and the driving table

What an entity is

An entity is a shape: "start at this table, and include everything connected to it". The guide says an entity "consists of a driving table and related tables" (User Guide 10.5.10, p.114) From docs. The driving table is the starting point. TDM works out the other tables from the constraints that involve the driving table and adds them by default. You then put a filter on columns of the tables in the entity to say which rows you want.

Here is the same idea on ShopSphere. We choose CUSTOMERS as the driving table because the business question is "give me some customers and everything they did". ORDERS points at CUSTOMERS through customer_id, ORDER_ITEMS points at ORDERS through order_id, and ORDER_ITEMS points at PRODUCTS through product_code. (The first two links are in the shared ShopSphere model. The product_code link is my assumption for this example: in the source it is a column that names a product, so I treat it as a foreign key.)

Visual referenceInformatica TDM 10.5.10 User Guide, p.115, Figure (entity map for the Employee entity). What I saw on the rendered page: an orange box "EMPLOYEE" with a star icon on the left, three light blue boxes to its right (names truncated, starting EMPLOYEEADD..., EMPLOYEEBAN..., EMPLOYEEDET...) joined by thin arrows, and a fourth box BANKACCOUNTS to the right of the middle one. That is exactly our CUSTOMERS to ORDERS to ORDER_ITEMS picture, just with different tables.

The same sentence appears in the entity overview (p.114), so it is documented, but the guide does not explain how min and max behave in a pure subset run. I do not rely on them in the ShopSphere example.

Where the relationships come from: physical and logical constraints

TDM can only follow links it knows about. Those links are the constraints stored in the project (User Guide 10.5.10, p.107 to 109) From docs. Foreign keys that already exist in the database are physical. If the database does not declare a foreign key (common in older systems), you can accept one that data discovery inferred (see the Informatica discovery and profiling chapter), or add one by hand as a logical constraint. Adding keys in TDM does not change the source database (p.109). The guide's advice before you build an entity is: run data discovery to identify constraints, and add constraints to the source to define the child tables (p.116).

If a link is missing, the child table will not come along

If ShopSphere's database has no declared foreign key from ORDER_ITEMS.product_code to PRODUCTS, an entity built from CUSTOMERS would not include PRODUCTS at all. Order lines would then point at products that are not in the target. Fix it by adding the logical constraint first, then building the entity. (This is my worked consequence of the guide's rule that entities follow constraints.)

Creating an entity, step by step

This is the guide's procedure (User Guide 10.5.10, p.116 to 117) From docs, with the reason for each step.

  1. Open the project, then click Define > Data Subset.

    The Define area is where all the "design" objects live: subset components here, masking assignments in Define | Data Masking (see the Informatica masking chapter).

    You see a list of entities and groups in the project, possibly empty.

  2. Click Actions > New > Entities.

    This starts the New Entity dialog.

  3. Enter a name and an optional description.

    Names matter later: plans list components by name. Something like ShopSphere_Customer_Entity beats Entity1.

  4. Click Select Table, choose the driving table, click OK.

    This is the key decision. Choose the table that answers your business question: "customers" for ShopSphere.

  5. Click Save.

    TDM now looks at the constraints of the driving table and adds every related table.

    A diagram of the tables and their relationships appears, and you can switch to the list views.

  6. To filter, click Edit in the Properties pane. The Edit Criteria dialog opens.

    Without a filter, the entity copies everything it reaches. The filter is what makes it a subset.

  7. On the Entity Criteria tab, click Add Criteria, select the columns to filter on, click OK.

    The columns you chose now appear in the list.

  8. For each column, pick the attribute: Value (type a value) or Parameter (pick a parameter). Pick an operator. Then type the value or choose the parameter. Click Save for each criterion.

    Parameters let you change the value later without editing the entity (13.7).

  9. Click Save to leave the Edit Criteria dialog.

    The entity is ready to add to a plan.

When you have several criteria in one entity the guide says the filter conditions act as "AND" (p.117). So customer_id <= 1002 and zip_code = '560001' together would keep only rows that satisfy both.

The guide's own small example (p.121) From docs: Employee is the driving table, Employee_History and Employee_Order have constraints to it, and the filter is EmpID = 10. After the plan runs, the target holds one Employee row for EmpID 10, all of that employee's history rows and all of their orders. That is the whole pattern. Replace Employee by CUSTOMERS and EmpID by customer_id and you have the ShopSphere example.

13.5 Groups, residual tables and templates

Groups

A group is "one or more unrelated tables" you want copied to the subset database. The guide gives two uses: add unrelated tables to a plan, or "copy unfiltered data to a target" (User Guide 10.5.10, p.116) From docs. When you add tables to a group you can include residual tables: tables that are not yet in any group or entity. Tick them when you want every table of the source to be in the subset plan.

Why would you need this? An entity only brings tables reachable by constraints from its driving table. ShopSphere's lookup tables (say, a table of country codes with no foreign keys) would be left behind. If the application needs them to start, put them in a group. The guide's own group example: an employee-skills table and an organization-location table are unrelated, you want both in the subset, so you put them in one group and add the group to the plan (p.116).

Creating a group

  1. In the project, open Define | Data Subset and click Actions > New > Groups.

    Same place as entities, different menu item (User Guide 10.5.10, p.123).

  2. In the Create Group dialog, enter a name and optional description, then Next.
  3. Click Add Tables, optionally filter the list to search, select tables, click OK.

    The filter box helps when a schema has hundreds of tables.

  4. Click Save.

    The group appears in the Define | Data Subset list next to your entities. Editing a group later lets you add or remove tables (p.124).

Templates: what the guide actually says

The guide mentions templates only in passing. It describes a template as "an optional component that contains the entities and groups" (User Guide 10.5.10, p.32, in the process overview, and again on p.293) From docs, and the Add Subset Components dialog lists "entities, groups, templates" together. In User Guide chapter 6 itself, which is about creating subsets, there is no section on creating a template. I could not find step-by-step instructions for templates in the guide excerpt I had, so I will not invent any. If your course uses templates, ask your trainer for the exact screens. Treat a template as a convenient way to bundle an entity and a group so you can add them to a plan in one go (that last sentence is my reading of the one-line definition, Unverified beyond it).

Entity or group? A quick rule

Ask: "Do I want rows filtered by a key and followed through relationships?" Yes means entity. "Do I just want the whole table as it is?" Yes means group.

13.6 How related rows are pulled

This is the part that makes subsetting more than a filter. When your filter selects customer 1001, which other rows must come with it? The answer depends on the constraint severity and on the data integrity option you pick in the plan. Both are covered in the guide (User Guide 10.5.10, p.108 to 109, 119 to 123).

Major and minor constraints

When a foreign key is defined, you give it a severity. The guide's rules From docs:

  • Major constraints move in both directions, child to parent and parent to child. A selected child pulls in its parent, and the parent then pulls in all of its children. This keeps referential and transactional integrity (p.108).
  • Minor constraints move one way only, child to parent. The parent row comes along, but the parent's other children do not (p.108 to 109).
  • You define the constraint on the child table. Severity does not change the entity's structure. You can change it after the entity exists, and also at run time in a subset plan, which leaves the entity unchanged (p.108).
  • By default every constraint in an entity is major (p.119).

The guide's own example (p.108 to 109) uses Customer as parent and Leads as child, with a filter on Leads that keeps only high-quality leads. With a major constraint, the subset receives each qualifying lead, its customer, and all leads of that customer. With a minor constraint it receives only the qualifying leads and their customers.

Now the same on ShopSphere. Suppose the filter is on ORDERS and keeps orders over 1000 in value. Major ORDERS-to-CUSTOMERS: you get those orders, their customers, and every other order of those customers. Minor: you get those orders and their customers only.

MAJOR (both ways)                     MINOR (child to parent only)

 CUSTOMER  1001                        CUSTOMER  1001
   |  ^                                  ^
   v  |  parent pulls ALL its children   |  parent comes along,
 ORDER 5001 (selected)                   |  siblings do not
 ORDER 5002 (comes along)              ORDER 5001 (selected)
 ORDER 5003 (comes along)              ORDER 5002 (left out)

The loop that surprises beginners

With major relations the pulling can chain. The guide describes it (p.108, p.122): a selected child record pulls in its parents; each parent then selects all its related children; those children might point to other parents not yet in the subset, which pull in more children, "in a loop until there are no more related records to select". This is how a small filter can grow into a big subset. You will see exactly this in the ShopSphere example when PRODUCTS is related by a major constraint (13.11).

For schemas with cycles, or objects that have more than one major parent, TDM uses recursive evaluation to compute the subset, and that needs a staging connection. The guide recommends using the source connection as the staging connection (p.109).
