Production is full of real people's private details, which is why teams cannot simply copy it into testing (the why-TDM chapter). Before any tool can protect data, someone has to say what "sensitive" means, which laws care about it, and how a tool can recognise it among thousands of columns.

This is the vocabulary layer of TDM. Discovery, subsetting, masking and all three tools use the words defined here: PII, PHI, PCI, policy, rule, data domain, classification.

What you will learn
  • What "sensitive data" means, and the three families you will hear about all the time: PII, PHI and PCI.
  • The difference between direct and indirect identifiers, and why removing names is not enough to make data anonymous.
  • What GDPR and HIPAA are in plain words, who they apply to, and the ideas behind them (consent, minimum necessary, de-identification). Other laws are mentioned only in general terms.
  • How data is classified by sensitivity, and how a tool finds sensitive columns using column names, data patterns and lists of values.
  • What a TDM policy, rule and data domain are, with the exact structure Informatica uses and the matching Delphix words.
  • Who owns which decision: compliance officer, data steward, TDM engineer.
Not legal advice

The laws below are summarised in plain words so conversations at work are easier to follow. Real compliance decisions belong to the organisation's compliance officer and legal team.

4.1 Words to know first

Sensitive data
Any data that could hurt a person or a company if it leaked or was misused: identity details, health records, payment details, passwords, trade secrets. "Sensitive" is a business and legal judgement, not just a technical one.
PII (Personally Identifiable Information)
Data that identifies a person, alone or combined with other data. Examples: name, email, phone number, a government ID number, a home address.
PHI (Protected Health Information)
Health-related data that can be tied to a person: diagnoses, treatments, medical record numbers, insurance member IDs. Informatica describes its PHI pack as aimed at the healthcare and pharmaceutical industries (User Guide 10.5.10, p.79).
PCI (Payment Card Industry) data
Payment card details such as the card number and expiry date. Informatica describes its PCI pack as aimed at banking and finance (User Guide 10.5.10, p.80).
Identifier
A piece of data that points to one person. A direct identifier does it on its own (an SSN). An indirect identifier (also called a quasi-identifier) only does it when combined with others (zip code plus birth date).
Re-identification
Working out who a "nameless" record belongs to by combining it with other information.
Data classification
Sorting data into sensitivity levels (for example public, internal, confidential, restricted) so each level gets the right protection.
Data domain
A named kind of data, such as "Social Security number" or "First name", described by how the data looks and what its column is usually called. Both Informatica and Delphix use this word, with slightly different meanings.
Rule
In Informatica, the logic that changes a value: a masking rule uses a masking technique (such as substitution) with chosen settings. Rules are covered in the masking concepts chapter.
Policy
In TDM, a named bundle that says which kinds of data are sensitive and how to protect each kind. Section 4.8 shows the exact Informatica structure.
Data steward
The person who knows a data set's meaning and quality and answers questions such as "what is in this column and who may see it?".
Compliance officer
The person responsible for making sure the organisation follows laws and internal rules about data.

Ask one question of each column: if this leaked, could someone be harmed or could ShopSphere be in trouble?

  • customer_id is just a number the system made up. On its own it harms nobody. But it links to orders, so it still needs careful handling (more in the masking concepts chapter).
  • first_name and last_name tell you who the person is.
  • email and phone let a stranger contact, spam, phish or impersonate the person.
  • ssn (a government ID number) is the classic example: with a name and a birth date it is enough to open credit in someone's name.
  • date_of_birth is used by banks and call centres to "prove" who you are.
  • zip_code looks harmless. Section 4.4 shows why it is not.
Why it matters in testing

Production data is protected by firewalls, access reviews and monitoring. A test database usually has none of that. Many people can log in, copies get passed around, and some environments live with outside vendors or in the cloud. If a real SSN sits in a test table, it carries production's legal weight into a place with a fraction of production's security. That mismatch is the core of the problem.

Delphix's own introduction makes the same point from the tool side: organisations face data breaches and pressure from regulators, and encryption helps protect data on disks and in transit but is, in their words, ill-suited for protecting non-production environments used for development, testing and reporting. Their answer is to replace confidential values with fictitious but realistic ones. From docs (Delphix "Introduction to Delphix Masking", source page ↗).

A quick test you can apply at work

Look at a column and ask three questions: (1) Does it identify a person? (2) Would a regulator or customer be upset if it leaked? (3) Would a tester still be able to do their job if it held a believable fake value instead? If the answers are yes, yes, yes, the column is a masking candidate. Question 3 matters: if the column cannot be faked without breaking the test, you have found a design problem to solve, not a reason to leave real data in place.
