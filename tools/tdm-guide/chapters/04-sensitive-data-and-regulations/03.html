4.5 Privacy laws and standards in plain words

This is not legal advice

Everything in this section is a plain-language orientation General concept, written so you can follow a meeting and ask good questions. Laws change, have exceptions, and apply differently depending on where your company and your customers are. Treat every statement here as "roughly true, check with legal". The tool documentation does not teach law, so none of this comes from the tool sources, except where marked.

Why should a tester care about laws? Because when a court or regulator looks at a data leak, they do not ask "was it production or test?". They ask "was personal data properly protected?". A copy in a test database is still personal data.

GDPR in plain words

General concept The GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) is a European Union law that has applied since May 2018.

  • What it protects: "personal data", meaning any information about an identifiable person. That is broader than a name and number: online identifiers and location data can count.
  • Who it applies to: organisations that handle the personal data of people in the EU, even if the organisation sits outside Europe. A ShopSphere with customers in Germany would be in scope no matter where its servers are.
  • Ideas behind it: there must be a lawful reason to use the data (consent is one such reason); use it only for the purpose it was collected for; collect and keep no more than needed ("data minimisation"); keep it secure; let people see and delete their data.
  • Fines at a high level: the most serious breaches can lead to fines of up to 20 million euros or 4 percent of worldwide yearly turnover, whichever is higher. Lesser breaches have a lower ceiling.

The consequence for test data. Two GDPR ideas hit TDM directly. First, purpose: a customer who gave details so ShopSphere could deliver an order did not necessarily agree to have them copied into a test lab. Second, minimisation and security: copying a whole production database to a loosely guarded test server is hard to justify when a small, masked copy would do. The law also treats truly anonymous data (nobody can be identified any more) as outside its scope, which is one reason masking and subsetting are popular. Whether a particular masked data set counts as anonymous is a question for your compliance officer.

HIPAA in plain words

General concept HIPAA is a United States law from 1996 that, through its Privacy and Security Rules, protects health information.

  • What it protects: PHI, health information that identifies a person (section 4.3).
  • Who it applies to: "covered entities" (health plans, healthcare providers, healthcare clearinghouses) and their "business associates", meaning vendors who handle PHI for them. A software company testing a hospital's billing system is typically a business associate.
  • Minimum necessary: people and systems should receive only the least PHI they need for their job. Testers rarely need the real thing.
  • De-identification: HIPAA describes two accepted ways to de-identify health data so it is no longer treated as PHI. One removes a defined list of 18 kinds of identifiers (names, detailed locations, most date elements except the year, phone numbers, record numbers and so on). The other has a qualified expert certify that the risk of identifying anyone is very small. This is why Delphix has a domain called HIPAA_DATE and why dates and ZIP codes feature so heavily in PHI discussions.
  • Penalties at a high level: civil penalties in tiers depending on how careless the organisation was, and criminal penalties for knowing misuse.

From docs Both tools mention these regimes only briefly. Delphix's introduction lists HIPAA, PCI and GDPR as examples of mandates organisations must stay compliant with, and Informatica packages its PHI and PCI policy packs for healthcare and banking respectively (User Guide 10.5.10, pp.79 to 80). GenRocket's column-profiling page says that identifying PII early helps organisations meet requirements "such as HIPAA".

4.6 Data classification: how sensitive is each column?

Data classification means labelling data by how much damage a leak could do, so that you can apply protection in proportion. Organisations choose their own scheme. A typical four-level scheme General concept looks like this:

A typical four-level classification (illustrative; your organisation's levels may differ)
LevelMeaningShopSphere exampleTypical test-data action
PublicSafe for anyone to seePRODUCTS.product_name, PRODUCTS.priceCopy as is
InternalFine inside the company, not outsideORDERS.order_date, ORDERS.total_amountCopy as is, or lightly vary
ConfidentialHarms people or the business if leakedCUSTOMERS.email, phone, first_name, last_name, zip_codeMask with realistic replacements
RestrictedSerious legal or financial harmCUSTOMERS.ssn, tax_id, date_of_birth, card numbersMask strongly, or never move to test

Two things are worth noticing. First, classification is about columns (and sometimes whole tables), not rows. Second, the result of classifying is a plan: each level maps to an action. A column with no label gets no action, so unlabelled sensitive data is the dangerous kind.

How the tools record sensitivity

  • From docs Informatica. When you create a data domain you set a sensitivity level for all its columns, chosen from levels that the Administrator defines (User Guide 10.5.10, p.75). Separately, each column in a project has a Sensitive flag. The guide says the flag is "for reference only" and does not affect processing, and that assigning a data domain to a column marks it sensitive automatically (p.89). A different property, Restricted, does change behaviour: the integration service does not write a restricted column's value to the target (p.89).
  • From docs Delphix. A domain ties a category of sensitive data (the terminology page's example is social security numbers) to the way it should be secured. The profiler assigns the domain to a column and, with it, the domain's default masking algorithm (Delphix "Discovering your sensitive data").
Sensitive flag versus masking rule

In Informatica, marking a column "sensitive" does not mask it. It documents your decision. Masking happens only when a rule is assigned to the column and a plan runs (see the Informatica data masking chapter). Beginners often tick "sensitive" and think the job is done.

General concept Many laws treat some data as especially sensitive: ethnic origin, political opinions, religion, trade union membership, health, sexual orientation, biometrics. The Delphix ASDD Standard profile set has domains for these (for example ETHNICITY, RELIGIOUS_ORIEN, POLITICAL_ORIEN, UNION_MEMBERSHIP_NO, BIOMETRIC and MEDICAL_CODES), and the default masking method it shows for BIOMETRIC is NullValueLookup.
